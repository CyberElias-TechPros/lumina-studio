**Purpose.** This is the single reference for every CEA academic document: the course
specification, the course manual, the session lesson notes, the instructor guide, the practical
workbook, the assessment framework, the final project, the marking rubric, the student feedback
form and the LMS structure. Produce every course from this template; do not edit course documents
into house styles of their own.

**Design principle.** Every course has a defined outcome; every lesson teaches and practises a
specific skill; every student produces evidence they can do the work. Nothing in this system is
accredited, licensed or regulated by NBTE, NSQF, JAMB or any other body. CEA certificates state
what the student did, never an external qualification CEA has not been authorised to issue.

---

## 1. Course specification (per course)

The specification binds every other document. It contains:

1. Course title, code, department, level.
2. Duration, sessions, guided contact hours, expected independent practice hours.
3. Delivery method, cohort size, equipment and software.
4. Course description, purpose and intended audience.
5. Prerequisites and entry requirements.
6. Learning outcomes (observable, assessable — "By the end of the course, the student can…").
7. Competency map (skill → where taught → how assessed).
8. Session-by-session breakdown (session number, week, title, minutes, topics).
9. Practical exercises and the final project.
10. Assessment methods, weights and grading scale.
11. Completion requirements and reassessment rules.
12. Further-learning recommendations.

Course codes: `CEA-<DEPT>-<NNN>` — OFF (Office & Data), CRE (Creative & Media), WEB (Web & Code),
BIZ (Business & Teaching), HDW (Computer Repairs), SEC (Cybersecurity). Codes are internal, not
external qualification codes.

Editorial rules: beginner-friendly English; technical terms explained, not avoided; A4-friendly
tables; diagrams and screenshots carry captions; documents remain useful printed in black and white;
no colour-only signalling; fonts embedded; page numbers, code and version in the footer.

## 3. Lesson note structure (per session — mandatory 14 parts)

1. **Lesson identification** — course, code, session number, week, title, duration, instructor.
2. **Learning outcomes** — what students should be able to demonstrate at the end.
3. **Prerequisite knowledge** — what must already be understood.
4. **Introduction** — a relatable situation, problem or question.
5. **Key concepts** — definitions, explanations, terminology.
6. **Instructor demonstration** — step-by-step, with expected results.
7. **Guided practice** — students repeat with support.
8. **Independent practice** — students complete a similar task unaided.
9. **Real-world application** — an office, school, business, church or household scenario.
10. **Assessment** — questions or a practical check.
11. **Common mistakes and troubleshooting** — typical errors, causes and fixes.
12. **Lesson summary** — the points to remember.
13. **Assignment** — defined task due before the next session.
14. **Reflection and progress** — what the student can now do, what to practise, what comes next.

Visual elements used consistently: **Key Concept**, **Remember**, **Practical Task**,
**Instructor Demonstration**, **Common Mistake**, **Real-World Example**, **Knowledge Check**,
**Assignment**, **Further Learning**. Screenshots carry a caption and an explanation of what the
student is looking at.

## 4. Instructor guide (per course)

- Course objectives and instructor responsibilities.
- Preparation checklist: room, machines, accounts, files, tested demos.
- Lesson delivery sequence per session (default 105 minutes):
  `0–10 welcome/attendance/review · 10–25 topic · 25–45 demonstrate · 45–70 guided practice ·
70–95 independent practice · 95–110 assessment and corrections · 110–120 summary and assignment`
- Required equipment and files; known failure points.
- Common misconceptions; suggested questions; expected answers.
- Differentiation: students who need more practice vs those progressing faster.
- Assessment instructions and marking guidance; moderation notes.
- Closure and next-session preparation.

Instructors do not teach from the student note alone. If a demo cannot be delivered from the guide,
the guide is wrong — fix it before the cohort.

## 5. Practical workbook

The workbook is the practice engine. Every activity includes:

1. Activity number and title. 2. Skill developed. 3. Scenario or task.
2. Equipment and software. 5. Starting files (if any). 6. Instructions.
3. Expected output. 8. Quality checklist. 9. Instructor feedback space. 10. Completion status.

Exercise types: follow-along, guided, independent, troubleshooting, scenario, timed, project,
peer review. Nigerian scenarios by default (provision store, church, school, SME, NGO, hospitality,
treatment of real data as fictional/anonymised only).

## 6. Assessment framework

Recommended weighting for short courses (course-specific deviations must be justified in the
specification):

| Component                               | Weight   |
| --------------------------------------- | -------- |
| Knowledge checks and quizzes            | 10%      |
| Practical exercises                     | 25%      |
| Assignments                             | 15%      |
| Final practical project                 | 40%      |
| Practical demonstration and explanation | 10%      |
| **Total**                               | **100%** |

Grading scale: 80–100 A · 70–79 B · 60–69 C · 50–59 D · 0–49 not yet competent. **Minimum pass 50%,
plus mandatory completion of critical practical requirements** (e.g. safe handling), which cannot be
average-waived. Reassessment once per component within two weeks of feedback, capped at one grade
boundary up. Feedback states: what was done correctly, what needs improvement, which outcomes were
achieved/not yet demonstrated, what to practise next, reassessment decision.

Academic integrity: cite sources, own the work, AI assistance disclosed and understood; a student
must be able to explain anything they submit. Copying in practical assessments = not competent.

## 7. Final project and portfolio

Every core course ends in an artefact a student can show. The project brief defines: purpose,
deliverables, submission format, deadline, tools allowed, marking rubric reference, and what the
observer checks in the live demonstration. Portfolio = the projects plus feedback and completion
record, downloadable/shareable without private data.

## 8. Marking rubrics

Rubrics are observable: each criterion names what the assessor sees (Yes / Not yet for competency
sheets; 0–4 descriptors for projects). Include calibration examples in the instructor guide so two
instructors mark the same work the same way.

## 9. Student feedback form

Collect at course end: clarity, usefulness, instructor effectiveness, practical activities,
equipment/facilities, duration, fairness, confidence gained, improvements. Used to fix the next
edition, not to collect testimonials.

## 10. LMS structure

Each course maps onto: Overview → Announcements → Roadmap → Modules (sessions) → Lessons →
Downloadable notes and practice files → Assignments → Quizzes → Project workspace → Results →
Feedback → Completion status. Progress and competency records live in the LMS only; never in this
tree. Offline-first: compressed videos, downloadable notes, text instructions, resumable lessons.

## 11. Quality assurance before publication

Ask of every lesson: accurate? aligned to an outcome? beginner-understandable? contains a practical
activity? deliverable in the allocated time? software instructions tested? examples appropriate?
images/resources legally usable? assessment aligned? skill independently demonstrable? If several
answers are "no", the lesson is not ready.
